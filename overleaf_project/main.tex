\begin{abstract}
SMOTE-derived oversamplers choose interpolation partners and place new points without regard to which features separate the classes. This paper examines whether feature attributions can change this. Attribution-Constrained SMOTE (AC-SMOTE) takes SHAP values from a Random Forest surrogate, uses them to weight the neighbor-search metric, and shortens the interpolation step along features with high attribution. It is compared with seven baselines on ten datasets (imbalance ratio 2.8 to 578) and three classifiers, using repeated cross-validation, Friedman and Wilcoxon tests, and Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg correction. AC-SMOTE improves \gmean{} and balanced accuracy over no resampling (Holm $p$ between 0.014 and 0.027), but the other oversamplers do so as well. Against SMOTE, the median \gmean{} difference is below 0.002 for every classifier, no raw-feature win over a SMOTE-family baseline other than ADASYN on AUC-PR with XGBoost survives Holm correction, and equivalence tests bound the \gmean{} difference to $\pm0.02$ for three of four settings. In an exploratory standardized rerun, the lowest mean rank reached with Logistic Regression on raw features does not hold: AC-SMOTE falls behind SMOTE on 9 of 10 datasets (Holm $p=0.029$). No attribution source, SHAP included, is significantly better than a uniform weight, and tuning, fidelity and calibration checks show no advantage. Overall, attribution-guided constraints showed no evidence of benefit over SMOTE.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Classifiers trained on imbalanced data tend to favor the majority class, because ignoring a rare class costs the training objective little. This matters most in fraud detection, medical screening and failure prediction, where a missed minority case costs more than a false alarm \cite{he2009learning}. Data-level remedies do not depend on the learner; the best known is SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote}, which creates a synthetic minority point on the segment between a minority instance and one of its $k$ nearest minority neighbors. Most later variants \cite{fernandez2018smote} change which instances serve as seeds or clean the data afterwards, but the interpolation step stays the same for every feature.

This uniformity may matter when only a few features separate the classes: a long step along them can carry a synthetic point toward the majority region. This paper studies whether surrogate-model attributions can restrain the step along such features. The resulting method, Attribution-Constrained SMOTE (\acs{}), uses TreeSHAP attributions \cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local} for a weighted neighbor-search distance and a per-feature reduction of the interpolation step. Feature-weighted neighbor search has been proposed before \cite{maldonado2022fw}. A web search in October 2026 (SHAP, attribution, explainability and feature importance combined with SMOTE and oversampling) found no method that restricts the interpolation step using model-derived attributions; the search was not exhaustive, so the novelty claim is hedged.

The contributions are: (i)~a formal definition of \acs{} (Section~\ref{sec:method}); (ii)~a leakage-free comparison with seven baselines on ten datasets and three classifiers, with Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg corrections side by side (Sections~\ref{sec:setup} and~\ref{sec:results}); (iii)~tests of the method's assumptions (uniform-control ablation, tuning, standardization, fidelity, calibration and cost-sensitive checks); and (iv)~equivalence tests bounding the advantage over SMOTE (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). The outcome is largely negative.

\section{Related Works}
\textit{Oversampling.} Borderline-SMOTE \cite{han2005borderline} generates points near the class boundary, ADASYN \cite{he2008adasyn} favors harder minority instances, SVM-SMOTE \cite{nguyen2011svm} uses a support vector classifier to find borderline instances, SMOTE-Tomek \cite{batista2004study} removes Tomek links afterwards, and K-means SMOTE \cite{douzas2018kmeans} clusters the data first. These methods change seed choice or post-processing; none shortens the step along particular features. Large comparisons \cite{kovacs2019empirical} find rankings depend on dataset and classifier, as here.

\textit{Feature weighting and attribution.} FW-SMOTE \cite{maldonado2022fw} scores feature relevance and redundancy and uses a weighted Minkowski distance for minority neighborhoods; interpolation is unchanged. \acs{} takes its weights from SHAP values \cite{lundberg2017unified} of a surrogate and also shortens the step. TreeSHAP \cite{lundberg2020local} gives exact attributions for tree ensembles in polynomial time, which is affordable inside every fold. Attribution is normally applied to a trained model; here it shapes the training set.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Problem Statement and Assumptions}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ with $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, where label 1 is the minority class, $n_{\min}=|\{i:y_i=1\}|$ and $n_{\max}=n-n_{\min}$. A resampler returns all original points plus $n_{\max}-n_{\min}$ synthetic minority points (1:1 balance). Assumed: (a)~numeric features; (b)~the resampler sees only the training partition of each fold, which includes the surrogate; and (c)~synthetic points carry the minority label. Neither a tree-based classifier nor standardized features are assumed; the latter matters in practice (Section~\ref{sec:std}).

\subsection{Attribution-Constrained SMOTE}\label{sec:method}
\acs{} keeps the seed--neighbor--interpolate structure of SMOTE and changes the last two steps using an attribution vector $\hat{w}\in[0,1]^d$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:acs}).

\textit{Phase 1: attribution vector.} A Random Forest surrogate \cite{breiman2001random} with 50 trees and maximum depth 6 is fitted to the training fold, without class weighting. TreeSHAP gives the matrix $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\min}\times d}$ of SHAP values for the minority-class output on the minority instances. The global importance of feature $j$ is
\begin{equation}
w_j=\frac{1}{n_{\min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\min}}|\Phi_{i,j}|,
\end{equation}
and $w$ is min--max normalized to $\hat{w}$, so that the most important feature has weight 1 and the least important weight 0 in every fold. If all $w_j$ are equal, $\hat{w}_j=0.5$.

\textit{Phase 2: attribution-weighted neighbor search.} For minority points $u,v$,
\begin{equation}
d_w(u,v)=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl(1+\alpha\hat{w}_j\bigr)(u_j-v_j)^2},
\end{equation}
with $\alpha\ge 0$. The $k$ nearest minority neighbors of every minority point are found from the full $n_{\min}\times n_{\min}$ matrix of $d_w$.

\textit{Phase 3: constrained interpolation.} A seed $x_i$ is drawn uniformly from the minority class and a neighbor $x_{nn}$ uniformly from its $k$ neighbors. With $\lambda\sim U(0,1)$, the per-feature step is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_j=\lambda\,(1-\beta\hat{w}_j),\qquad x^{\mathrm{synth}}_j=x_{i,j}+\lambda_j\,(x_{nn,j}-x_{i,j}),
\end{equation}
with $\beta\in[0,1]$. For $\beta\in[0,1]$ the factor $1-\beta\hat{w}_j$ lies in $[0,1]$, so no clipping of $\lambda_j$ is needed. The defaults are $k=5$ (the SMOTE default), $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=0.7$, moderate nominal values (a distance weight of at most 3, a step shortened by at most 70\%) used unchanged for every dataset and classifier unless stated otherwise. Sweeps and nested tuning are in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]\small
\caption{AC-SMOTE}\label{alg:acs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE training fold $(X,y)$; $k,\alpha,\beta$
\STATE $X_{\min}\leftarrow$ minority rows; $m\leftarrow n_{\max}-n_{\min}$
\STATE fit surrogate RF on $(X,y)$; $\Phi\leftarrow$ TreeSHAP$(X_{\min})$
\STATE $w_j\leftarrow \mathrm{mean}_i|\Phi_{ij}|$; $\hat{w}\leftarrow$ min--max$(w)$
\STATE $D\leftarrow \big[d_w(u,v)\big]_{u,v\in X_{\min}}$; $N\leftarrow k$ nearest columns per row
\FOR{$t=1$ \TO $m$}
\STATE draw $i$, $nn\in N_i$, $\lambda\sim U(0,1)$
\STATE $x^{(t)}_j\leftarrow x_{i,j}+\lambda(1-\beta\hat{w}_j)(x_{nn,j}-x_{i,j})$ for all $j$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $(X\cup\{x^{(t)}\},\; y\cup\{1\}^m)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textit{Properties.} Four properties bear on how the results should be read.

\emph{(P1) Reduction.} With $\alpha=\beta=0$, $d_w$ is Euclidean and $\lambda_j=\lambda$ for all $j$, so \acs{} has the same generating process as SMOTE; on a synthetic set the mean and covariance differences from SMOTE matched those between two SMOTE seeds (0.24 vs.\ 0.23 and 0.83 vs.\ 0.83).

\emph{(P2) Bounded displacement.} For every $j$, $x^{\mathrm{synth}}_j$ lies between $x_{i,j}$ and $x_{nn,j}$, and $|x^{\mathrm{synth}}_j-x_{i,j}|\le(1-\beta\hat{w}_j)\,|x_{nn,j}-x_{i,j}|$. On the most important feature the largest step is therefore $1-\beta$ of SMOTE's. Because the $\lambda_j$ differ, the point lies in the box spanned by seed and neighbor but, unlike SMOTE, not on the segment joining them.

\emph{(P3) Uniform weights.} If $\hat{w}_j=c$ for all $j$, neighbor sets equal the Euclidean ones and every step is scaled by $1-\beta c$. A uniform control removes feature-specific weighting but keeps a global step shortening ($c=0.5$ in the ablation: 35\%).

\emph{(P4) Scale dependence.} Since $1+\alpha\hat{w}_j\le 1+\alpha$, attribution can change a feature's contribution to the squared distance by a factor of at most 3 at $\alpha=2$. Differences in units between raw features are often far larger, so on unstandardized data the neighbor search is governed mainly by units; like SMOTE's, $d_w$ is not scale-invariant.

\textit{Cost.} Phase~1 costs one forest fit plus TreeSHAP, $O(TLD^2)$ per explained instance \cite{lundberg2020local}; Phase~2 costs $O(n_{\min}^2 d)$ time and $O(n_{\min}^2)$ memory, which limits very large minority classes; Phase~3 costs $O(md)$ for $m$ generated points.

The seven baselines are no resampling, random oversampling (ROS), SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN, SVM-SMOTE and SMOTE-Tomek, all from imbalanced-learn with default settings and a fixed random state, balancing the training fold 1:1. \subsection{Protocol and Statistics}\label{sec:protocol}
Repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 3 repeats (seed 42) gives 15 folds for every dataset--classifier--method cell. In every fold the split comes first, the resampler is fitted on the training part only, and the test part is never resampled. The scores are minority-class F1, AUC-PR (average precision), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), \gmean{} \cite{kubat1997addressing} and balanced accuracy; all but AUC-PR use the default decision rule. Fold scores are averaged within each dataset before any test. Following Dem\v{s}ar \cite{demsar2006statistical}, the Friedman test is applied across the eight methods (10 datasets as blocks), with Nemenyi critical difference $\mathrm{CD}=3.32$ ($k=8$, $N=10$, $\alpha=0.05$) for critical-difference diagrams, and \acs{} is compared with each baseline by a two-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with pairwise adjustment as discussed by Garc\'ia and Herrera \cite{garcia2008extension}. A family consists of the seven comparisons for one setting (LR, RF, XGB or standardized LR) and one metric, giving 20 families; Holm \cite{holm1979simple} (family-wise error rate) and Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) \cite{benjamini1995controlling} (false discovery rate) adjustments are applied within families only, and both are reported. With $N=10$ the smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is 0.002, or 0.0137 after Holm adjustment over seven comparisons. A cost-sensitive family is the five metrics for one classifier, and a fidelity family the six comparisons with the other oversamplers for one classifier. Tuning, fidelity and calibration use one-sided tests in favor of \acs{}, the first and last unadjusted. The raw-feature comparisons with the seven baselines are the main analysis; the standardized LR rerun, added after the raw-feature results had been seen, and the seed, ablation, tuning, fidelity, calibration, cost-sensitive and equivalence analyses are exploratory.

Absence of significance is not evidence of equivalence. \acs{} is therefore also compared with SMOTE on dataset-level \gmean{} (and balanced accuracy, in the supplement) by two one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (TOST) \cite{schuirmann1987comparison,lakens2017equivalence} at margins of $\pm0.01$ and $\pm0.02$, a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval for the median difference (20\,000 resamples), and the minimum difference detectable at 80\% power by a paired $t$-test with the observed standard deviation of the differences (an approximation at $N=10$). The margins were chosen after the data had been seen and are illustrative; TOST $p$-values are Holm-adjusted across the four settings. Code, configurations, per-fold results and supplementary tables are available at \url{https://github.com/udayraj1238/Explanation-Guided-Synthetic-Oversampling-for-Imbalanced-Classification}.

\subsection{Feature Standardization (Exploratory)}\label{sec:std}
LR is scale-sensitive, and so are Euclidean neighbor searches (P4). The LR experiment was repeated (seed 42) with a standard scaler fitted on each training fold before resampling (LR-std in Tables~\ref{tab:ranks} and~\ref{tab:pairwise}). The mean \gmean{} rank of \acs{} moves from 3.00 to 5.00, behind SMOTE (2.55) and SMOTE-Tomek (2.35). It beats SMOTE on 1 of 10 datasets (median difference $-0.0019$, Holm $p=0.029$) and SMOTE-Tomek on none (Holm $p=0.014$). The deficit is small (about 0.002 in median \gmean{}) but consistent in sign. A possible, untested explanation (P4): on raw features the attribution weights cannot compete with differences in units, whereas after standardization they are the only feature-dependent element of the neighbor search.

\subsection{Attribution Fidelity, Calibration and Cost-Sensitive Learning}
\textit{Attribution fidelity.} The Spearman correlation $\rho$ between SHAP feature rankings of models trained on the raw and the resampled fold was computed on up to 500 test rows (RF: 100 trees, depth 10; XGB: 100 trees); it does not measure boundary geometry. ROS gave the highest $\rho$ (0.807 for RF, 0.778 for XGB); \acs{} (0.725, 0.632) matched SMOTE (0.729, 0.630), and its nominal advantage over ADASYN on RF (one-sided $p=0.0098$) did not survive Holm correction ($p=0.059$).

\textit{Calibration.} On five datasets, no oversampler had a lower Brier score than None for any classifier. For LR (standardized) the Brier score rose from 0.042 (None) to 0.085 (\acs{}) and 0.083 (SMOTE), and the expected calibration error from 0.023 to 0.114 for \acs{}: the degradation comes from 1:1 oversampling, not from \acs{}; for RF and XGB the Brier scores of \acs{} and SMOTE differ by at most 0.001 (0.049 vs.\ 0.049, 0.048 vs.\ 0.047). No comparison favoring \acs{} was significant (smallest one-sided $p=0.0625$, against a floor of 0.031 at $n=5$), so these tests cannot detect a small advantage.

\textit{Cost-sensitive learning.} Against class-weighted LR, RF and XGB \cite{elkan2001foundations} on raw features, \acs{} had higher F1 and MCC on 9 of 10 datasets for LR (BH $p=0.049$; Holm $p=0.068$, 0.078) but not higher \gmean{} (4 wins, 6 losses), as did SMOTE; for RF and XGB nothing was significant after correction.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
Timing is the wall-clock time of the resample-and-fit stage, so it includes classifier training (SVM-SMOTE and SMOTE-Tomek are omitted because only their resampling was timed). Over the nine datasets other than creditcard, \acs{} took 1.9, 6.2 and 1.6 s per fold with LR, RF and XGB, against 0.74, 5.0 and 0.69 s for SMOTE; the extra 1.1, 1.2 and 0.9 s is consistent with the surrogate fit and SHAP, which were not timed separately. On creditcard the times are dominated by classifier training (None alone: 15, 258 and 6 s), vary strongly with the classifier and resampled data (RF: 230 s for \acs{} vs.\ 657 s for SMOTE) and were taken without controlling machine load, so the lower \acs{} time there (144 s vs.\ 238 s) says nothing about resampling cost, which was not timed in isolation.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
In every setting tested, \acs{} performed like SMOTE: median \gmean{} differences of at most 0.002 and a small consistent deficit with standardized LR. Neither weighted neighbor search nor step shortening gave a measurable benefit. Plausible reasons, none tested, are that reweighting moves the $k=5$ neighbor set little when the minority class is small, that the shorter step makes synthetic points near-duplicates of their seeds, and that raw scale dominates the distance (P4); neighbor-set overlap with Euclidean $k$-NN and synthetic-to-seed distance were not measured.

Limitations: (1)~Ten datasets give limited power (minimum detectable \gmean{} difference 0.003 to 0.023), and the equivalence margins were chosen post hoc. (2)~Raw features let units dominate the neighbor search (P4), confounding method with scale; the standardized rerun covers LR only (RF and XGB are scale-invariant, their resamplers are not), with one seed. (3)~The surrogate is a Random Forest fitted on the imbalanced fold without class weighting, with SHAP computed on minority instances only; with 32 to 41 minority instances (abalone\_19, ecoli, oil) the attributions are likely unstable across folds (not measured), and min--max normalization stretches near-uniform attributions over $[0,1]$, possibly amplifying noise. Linear and class-weighted surrogates were not tried. (4)~Ablation, sweeps and fidelity use tree ensembles only (ablation and sweeps: XGB, five datasets), and the calibration run standardizes LR whereas the main grid does not. (5)~FW-SMOTE, K-means SMOTE and generative oversamplers are not baselines, and baselines run at defaults whereas \acs{} was tuned separately. (6)~Scores use the default decision rule, with no threshold-moving or balanced-ensemble baseline, and 1:1 balancing lowers precision (LR on creditcard: F1 0.73 without resampling, 0.15 with SMOTE). (7)~All datasets are tabular and binary, only one is large, one-hot features sit awkwardly with Euclidean distance, and only 1:1 balancing was studied. For practitioners, the results suggest plain SMOTE, or another baseline, unless attribution-guided generation has a specific justification, and standardized features if \acs{} is used.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper defined AC-SMOTE, which uses SHAP attributions from a surrogate to weight neighbor search and shorten the interpolation step along informative features, and compared it with seven baselines on ten datasets and three classifiers. It improves on no resampling, as every oversampler does. Relative to SMOTE and the other SMOTE-family baselines it shows no significant advantage after correction, and the equivalence analysis rules out a median \gmean{} advantage above about 0.008. Its best raw-feature result, the lowest mean rank with Logistic Regression, reverses under a change of seed and under standardization, and a uniform control performs about as well as SHAP. Future work should test the explanations in Section~\ref{sec:disc}, repeat the ablation and tuning for linear models, and add FW-SMOTE, K-means SMOTE, threshold-tuned and balanced-ensemble baselines.
